    Where they really are

    Portolan charts are the first maps that look right. Drawn in ink on a single animal skin from the late 1200s, criss-crossed with compass lines and crowded with harbour names written at right angles to the coast, they show the Mediterranean and the Black Sea with a shape you would recognise at a glance, two centuries before anyone drew latitude lines on a sea chart. Nobody knows quite how they were made. I wanted to know how good they actually are, so I read {{counts.pisana.n|int}} places on the oldest one to survive, the Carta Pisana (about 1275), and {{counts.catalan.n|int}} on the chart of the Catalan Atlas (1375), and measured every one against the real coast.

    Above, the left map is the chart itself. The red lines are the true meridians and parallels every 5 degrees, bent to follow the drawing: where they run straight and evenly spaced the chart is faithful, where they kink it is not. Slide the bar to morph the real coastline from the chart's shape to its own best straight frame, and tap any ring to see the same place on the modern ink map on the right. Use the chips to switch charts.

    01 · The tiltTen degrees off north

    Fit either chart to the real coast with a single scale and a single turn, the simplest possible "best match", and the turn is not zero. The Carta Pisana comes out rotated {{fit.pisana.rot_deg|f1}} degrees anticlockwise, the Catalan Atlas {{fit.catalan.rot_deg|f1}} degrees. You can see it in the grid on the map above: the parallels climb from west to east. A ship sailing due east from Gibraltar would, on these charts, be heading for a coast that is drawn too far north, which is why the Levant sits high and the Strait of Gibraltar low.

    The same tilt shows in directions. Over {{fit.pisana.bearing.pairs|int}} pairs of Pisana places at least 100 km apart, the direction on the chart is a median {{fit.pisana.bearing.median_abs_deg|f1}} degrees off the true compass bearing, anticlockwise for {{fit.pisana.bearing.neg_pct|pc}} of them. Turn the chart back by its fitted rotation and the median error falls to {{fit.pisana.bearing.median_abs_corrected_deg|f1}} degrees; {{fit.pisana.bearing.within5_corrected_pct|pc}} of pairs are then within 5 degrees. For the Catalan Atlas the figures are {{fit.catalan.bearing.median_abs_deg|f1}} and {{fit.catalan.bearing.median_abs_corrected_deg|f1}} degrees. In other words the charts are not vague about direction: they are precise, but about a north that is not true north.

    The usual explanation is the magnetic compass. If the chart makers built their coasts from compass courses, and the compass pointed several degrees east of true north in the medieval Mediterranean, the whole drawing would be turned by that amount. Historians still argue about whether declination alone can explain a tilt this large; what the measurement shows is that the turn is about the same size on two charts made a century apart, and that on the Pisana the western and eastern basins, fitted separately, turn by almost the same amount ({{fit.pisana.basins.W Med.rot_deg|f1}} and {{fit.pisana.basins.E Med.rot_deg|f1}} degrees).

    02 · Sea by seaSuperb at home, lost in the Atlantic

    With the tilt taken out, the Carta Pisana places its Mediterranean harbours with a median error of {{fit.pisana.basins.W Med.global_median_km|int}} km in the west and {{fit.pisana.basins.E Med.global_median_km|int}} km in the east, on a map more than 3,000 km wide. Give each basin its own scale and turn and the errors fall to {{fit.pisana.basins.W Med.own_median_km|int}} km and {{fit.pisana.basins.E Med.own_median_km|int}} km. Judged against its eight nearest neighbours, which ignores how the pieces join, the median Pisana place is {{fit.pisana.local_median_km|int}} km out and the median Catalan place {{fit.catalan.local_median_km|int}} km; Capo Peloro by Messina, Almería and Sidon are within a few kilometres of where their neighbours say they should be.

    The Atlantic coasts are another matter. The Pisana's Biscay, Channel and Flemish coast are a median {{fit.pisana.basins.Atlantic.global_median_km|int}} km out, {{fit.pisana.atl_vs_med|f1}} times worse than the Mediterranean, and Britain is a schematic box. Even fitted on their own they come out {{atlantic.pisana.shrink_pct|pc}} too small against the rest of the chart. The Catalan Atlas, a century later, has a recognisable Ireland and Britain, Madeira and the Canaries, but its Atlantic places are still a median {{fit.catalan.basins.Atlantic.global_median_km|int}} km out, the worst being Dunnet Head at the tip of Scotland ({{places.catalan.dunnet_head.res_km|int}} km), and its Atlantic is drawn {{atlantic.catalan.shrink_pct|pc}} too small.

    03 · WhyCharts made from sailing

    The pattern fits the way the charts were used. The Mediterranean was sailed constantly by Genoese, Venetian, Pisan, Catalan and Majorcan ships, and the written sailing directions of the time (the portolani that gave the charts their name) list courses by compass wind and distances in miles between harbour after harbour. A chart built up from thousands of such courses, averaged and corrected over generations, would be good locally everywhere, a little stretched or turned from one basin to the next, and tilted as a whole by the compass. The Atlantic coasts beyond Gibraltar were sailed far less by the same people, the tides and long open crossings of Biscay make dead reckoning harder, and the chart makers seem to have fitted them on at the edges. Britain, which few Mediterranean pilots knew, stays a sketch.

    The grid tells the same story. A straight frame with equal degrees of longitude and latitude (scaled for 38° N) fits the Pisana a little better than Mercator ({{fit.pisana.proj.equirect_sim.median_km|int}} against {{fit.pisana.proj.mercator_sim.median_km|int}} km; Catalan Atlas {{fit.catalan.proj.equirect_sim.median_km|int}} against {{fit.catalan.proj.mercator_sim.median_km|int}} km), so on these readings there is no sign of a hidden Mercator-like construction, though over a sea that spans only 15 degrees of latitude the two are hard to tell apart.

    The distortion grid: the true 5 degree meridians and parallels, carried onto each chart by a smoothed thin-plate spline through my readings, within about 400 km of a place I read.

    04 · Against older mapsPtolemy and Hereford

    I have measured three other medieval and ancient world pictures the same way. Ptolemy's Geography of about 150 AD, a list of coordinates, puts its places a median {{compare.ptolemy_helmert_median_km|int}} km out after the same kind of best fit, about {{compare.ratio_ptolemy_pisana|f1}} times the Pisana's {{fit.pisana.median_km|int}} km, and region by region its Italy is {{compare.ptolemy_italy_km|int}} km out and its Greece {{compare.ptolemy_greece_km|int}} km, against {{fit.pisana.basins.W Med.own_median_km|int}} and {{fit.pisana.basins.E Med.own_median_km|int}} km for the Pisana's two basins. The Hereford Mappa Mundi, drawn within a few decades of the Pisana, misses by a median {{compare.hereford_median_km|int}} km, but it was never meant to be a map in this sense. Al-Idrisi's world map of 1154 gets directions right to a median {{compare.idrisi_bearing_deg|int}} degrees and its places within {{compare.idrisi_local_km|int}} km of their neighbours. Ptolemy covers a far larger world, so the comparison flatters the charts, but within the Mediterranean the portolans are a step change.

    Median error after the best fit, from my studies of each map. The measures differ in detail (see the paper); the order does not.

    05 · How I did thisReading by eye, fitting by numbers

    Readings. There is no open table of control points for these charts, so I read them myself, on public domain scans of the originals from the Bibliothèque nationale de France on Wikimedia Commons. On the Carta Pisana the ink is badly faded, so I read {{transfer.from_facsimile|int}} of the {{counts.pisana.n|int}} places on Jomard's 19th-century facsimile and carried each one back to the original through {{transfer.nodes|int}} points where the compass lines meet, which can be found on both images, and {{transfer.ties|int}} places I could read on both. Tested by leaving each of those places out, the transfer is accurate to about {{transfer.loo_median_km|int}} km, which is part of the Pisana's local error. The Catalan Atlas chart is four separate panels. I placed them side by side, and because the scan of the third panel draws Corsica, Sardinia and the coast of Tunis again, I moved the two eastern panels {{fit.catalan.rose_gap_px|int}} pixels west so that the repeated coasts line up. For each place I typed its modern position; {{counts.pisana.conf12_pct|pc}} of Pisana readings and {{counts.catalan.conf12_pct|pc}} of Catalan readings are clear or probable, the rest are certain places whose exact point I could not pin down.

    Fits. For each chart I fitted the true positions, in several projections, to the chart positions with one scale, one turn and one shift (a similarity fit), and with a general affine fit; the errors are great-circle kilometres between each true place and where the fit puts it. The local error predicts each place from an affine fit to its eight nearest neighbours. The distortion grid is a smoothed thin-plate spline from true positions to the chart, drawn only within about 400 km of a place I read.

    What this cannot say. The places are the ones I could read and identify, so they lean towards big harbours and clear headlands; the Pisana's Black Sea has only {{counts.pisana.basins.Black Sea|int}} readable places and is left out of the sea-by-sea figures. Identifications are mine. The scans are of uneven quality, and the Catalan Atlas folds hide part of the drawing; if instead the panels are allowed to slide freely the Catalan fit is no better ({{fit.catalan.free.median_km|int}} km). None of this changes the tilt or the gap between the Mediterranean and the Atlantic.
